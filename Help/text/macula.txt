foveal reflex/ A bright, glistening "white dot."
ILM reflex / A bright, pinpoint, or oval glint of light reflecting off the central macula (fovea). It is very sharp and prominent in children and young adults because their ILM is very smooth. It naturally fades or becomes "dull" with age.
Complete PVD / The Weiss Ring: This is the definitive clinical sign. It is a circular, translucent floating opacity that represents the detached attachment point from the optic nerve head.
Cystoid spaces /The observation of Cystoid Macular Edema (CME) on fundoscopy often shows a thickened macula with a loss of the normal foveal reflex. In advanced cases, fluid accumulates in a characteristic 'flower petal' (petaloid) pattern due to the arrangement of Henle’s nerve fibers.
Serous retinal detachment/ A serous retinal detachment often presents as a smooth, dome-shaped bubble. A major clinical sign is shifting fluid, where the detachment changes position based on the patient's head posture due to simple gravity.
Pigment Epithelial Detachment/Serous PED: Appears as a well-demarcated, dome-shaped elevation. It often has a translucent or orange-yellow hue and may "glow" or transilluminate when the light beam is placed over it. Drusenoid PED: Typically presents as a yellow-white elevation with scalloped or "irregular" borders. You might see a "triradiate" or stellate pattern of brownish pigment on its surface. Fibrovascular PED: Often looks more irregular and less "smooth" than serous types. Look for signs of activity like yellow exudates or subretinal hemorrhage (blood) at the margins
Drusen/ Yellow-white extracellular deposits that accumulate between the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and Bruch’s membrane. Hard Drusen: Small (<63 µm), round, and discrete with very sharp borders. Soft Drusen: Larger (>125 µm), amorphous, and pale yellow with fuzzy or indistinct borders. Numerous tiny, tiny "pinpoint" yellow lesions.
Hard exudates/Small, yellowish-white lipid deposits with sharp margins that form within the retinal layers. Unlike drusen (which are beneath the RPE), hard exudates are intraretinal, typically located in the outer plexiform layer.
Geographic Atrophy/Sharply Demarcated Areas: Patches of the retina appear as "islands" of depigmentation with very clear, well-defined borders. Visible Choroidal Vessels: Because the pigmented RPE layer is missing, you can easily see the larger, underlying choroidal blood vessels through the atrophic patch. Color Change: These areas typically appear lighter, pale pink, or yellowish-white compared to the surrounding healthy retina.
Macular nevus / Typically appears as a flat or minimally elevated, slate-gray to brown lesion located deep within the choroid. It is characterized by well-demarcated but soft borders that blend into the surrounding fundus, often reaching a diameter of several millimeters.
Subretinal hemorrhage/ bright red to deep maroon lesion with irregular, scalloped, or diffuse borders. Because the blood is located deep to the neurosensory retina, the retinal vasculature is clearly visible passing over the surface of the hemorrhage
Full Thickness Macular Hole (FTMH) / Appears as a well-defined, round, red spot in the center of the macula. A surrounding grayish halo or "cuff" indicates fluid that has seeped under the edges of the hole, causing a shallow local detachment.
Lamellar macular hole/ a partial-thickness defect where the inner layers of the fovea are lost or split, but the outer retinal layers (including photoreceptors) remain intact. IIt typically appears as a round or oval reddish lesion at the center of the macula, slightly darker than the surrounding tissue.

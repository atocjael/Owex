Pavingstone degeneration / Lesions appear as discrete, rounded, pale areas that resemble a stone-paved street. They often have a thin pigmented border and may show large choroidal blood vessels running underneath them. It is almost exclusively found in the far periphery of the retina, most frequently in the inferior (bottom) quadrants.

White without pressure (WWOP) / a common, typically benign peripheral retinal finding where a portion of the retina appears translucent white to gray. It often presents as a flat, whitish film with a sharply demarcated posterior border. This border sometimes has a reddish-brown or "dark" appearance, which can be mistaken for a retinal tear.

Lattice degeneration is a common peripheral retinal condition characterized by localized thinning of the retinal tissue, often accompanied by a "cross-hatching" pattern of whitened blood vessels. It is the most important visible lesion predisposing to retinal tears and detachment.

Vitreoretinal tufts (or retinal tufts) are small, congenital projections of glial and retinal tissue that extend into the vitreous cavity. They represent focal areas of abnormally strong adhesion between the retina and the vitreous gel. They typically appear chalky white or grayish-white. Unlike retinal tears, which are often translucent, tufts are opaque due to the presence of glial (scar-like) tissue.

Degenerative (Acquired/Senile) Retinoschisis / A smooth, dome-shaped, and transparent elevation of the inner retinal layers. It lacks the corrugations or surface wrinkling often seen in retinal detachments.

A retinal tear /full-thickness break in the neurosensory retina that typically occurs when the vitreous gel exerts acute traction on the retinal surface, often during a posterior vitreous detachment. On fundoscopy, the most characteristic finding is a horseshoe-shaped (U-shaped) flap of tissue, with its apex pointing toward the center of the eye and its base directed toward the periphery. 

Atrophic hole /  a small, well-circumscribed, and typically round reddish lesion in the peripheral retina. The red color is a result of the underlying choroidal vasculature being visible through the full-thickness retinal break.

A retinal detachment is a sight-threatening ocular emergency where the neurosensory retina separates from the underlying retinal pigment epithelium, cutting off its vital blood and nutrient supply. The detached retina typically appears as an elevated, opaque, grayish-white membrane that has lost its normal transparent, orange-red reflex. 

Peripheral Neovascularization / refers to the growth of fragile, abnormal new blood vessels in the outer edges of the retina, typically in response to ischemia.  Abnormal clusters or networks of new blood vessels growing at the vitreoretinal interface.

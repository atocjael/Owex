microaneurysms / Small, discrete red dots on the surface of the retina. They are typically the earliest visible sign of diabetic retinopathy. They look like tiny, round, or circular red dots. While most are bright red, some may appear yellowish if they have undergone internal changes like hyalinization.
Cotton wool spots / Also called "soft exudates",  they present as superficial, whitish-gray patches with fuzzy, ill-defined borders that resemble small tufts of cotton or clouds. Unlike hard exudates, they are opaque and slightly elevated because they occur in the nerve fiber layer, often appearing to obscure underlying retinal vessels. 
Dot and blot hemorrhages / Dark red, circular, or irregular "blobs" located deep within the retinal tissue. Their characteristic rounded shape occurs because the escaped blood is physically compressed by the densely packed neurons of the inner nuclear and outer plexiform layers.

Neovascularization elsewhere (NVE)/ Appears as delicate, disorganized "fronds" or lacy networks of tiny, irregular blood vessels branching from existing retinal veins. These new vessels are typically found more than one disc diameter away from the optic nerve head and often grow along the surface of the retina or extend into the vitreous.

Boat-shaped or preretinal hemorrhages / Large, distinct pools of bright red blood trapped between the retina and the vitreous (subhyaloid space). Because the blood is not confined by retinal tissue, gravity causes the red blood cells to settle at the bottom, creating a characteristic straight horizontal top border and a curved, rounded bottom.

Venous beading and looping / Structural venous deformities that signify severe retinal ischemia and a high risk of progression to proliferative disease. Venous beading is characterized by focal, irregular areas of dilation and narrowing, giving the vessel a "string of sausages" or beaded appearance, while venous looping occurs when a vein deviates into a sharp, omega-shaped or circular turn.

Intraretinal Microvascular Abnormalities (IRMA) / fine, tortuous, "wiry" red lines that form a complex, lacy network within the retinal tissue. These vessels are typically found in the mid-periphery, often adjacent to areas of capillary non-perfusion or near cotton wool spots, acting as intraretinal shunts. Unlike the delicate "fronds" of neovascularization, IRMA vessels have sharper, better-defined outlines, are slightly larger in caliber, and are strictly contained within the retina—they do not cross over major retinal vessels or grow into the vitreous.

retinal emboli /small, distinct particles trapped within the retinal arterioles, typically categorized into three main types based on their appearance and source. Cholesterol emboli (Hollenhorst plaques) are the most common and appear as highly refractile, bright yellow or orange crystals usually located at vessel bifurcations; they often originate from carotid artery atherosclerosis. Calcific emboli are dull, chalky white, and larger, typically lodging in major vessels near the optic disc and suggesting a source from calcified heart valves. Platelet-fibrin emboli present as dull, grayish-white, elongated "plugs" that may move through the circulation or appear in "showers," often associated with mural thrombi in the carotid system or heart.

infectious and inflammatory lesions (chorioretinitis) / typically present as creamy yellow or whitish patches on the retina, with their appearance varying significantly based on whether the condition is active or chronic. Active Lesions: These appear as creamy, yellowish-white patches with fuzzy, blurred borders due to surrounding retinal edema. They are often accompanied by "vitritis"—inflammatory cells in the vitreous that create a "haze" or "headlight in the fog" effect. Inactive/Healed Lesions: Chronic lesions develop into sharply demarcated scars with distinct borders. These scars often show pigmentation (black clumping) at the edges and areas of "punched-out" atrophy where the underlying choroidal vessels may become visible.

Flame-shaped hemorrhages / bright red, splinter-like streaks within the superficial nerve fiber layer (RNFL). 

choroidal nevus / a flat or minimally elevated, well-defined lesion with a characteristic slate-gray or brown color and feathered borders. Key indicators of a benign, long-standing lesion include the presence of overlying drusen and the absence of symptoms like flashes or vision loss. To differentiate it from a melanoma, clinicians look for "red flag" features such as a thickness greater than 2 mm, the presence of subretinal fluid, or the appearance of orange pigment (lipofuscin) on the surface.

A tigroid fundus (also called a tessellated fundus) refers to a striped or mosaic-like appearance of the eye's posterior. This color pattern occurs when the Retinal Pigment Epithelium (RPE) is thin or lacks enough pigment, allowing the darker, underlying choroidal blood vessels to become visible. It typically appears as dark, polygonal areas or stripes separated by the reddish/orange lines of the choroidal vessels.

A pale color of the retina often indicates either a loss of normal blood flow or an accumulation of abnormal materials within the retinal layers. This appearance is frequently as retinal opacification or whitening rather than simple "paleness"

A hazy fundus background refers to a loss of detail and clarity when viewing the back of the eye. This "haze" is typically caused by media opacities—cloudiness in the structures light must pass through to reach the retina—rather than a disease of the retina itself

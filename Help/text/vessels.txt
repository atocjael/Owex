The standard A/V ratio is typically 2:3. This means that a healthy retinal artery is roughly two-thirds the width of its accompanying vein. Arteries appear thinner and lighter in color (bright red), while veins are wider and darker (deep red)

Gunn sign (also known as arteriovenous nicking or nipping) is a hallmark of chronic hypertensive retinopathy. It is characterized by the tapering or apparent narrowing of a retinal vein on either side of the point where it is crossed by a thickened, sclerotic arteriole. This "pinched" appearance occurs because the hardened arterial wall, along with a shared connective tissue sheath, compresses the underlying vein and obscures the blood column within it.

Salus's sign is a clinical finding on fundoscopy where a retinal vein is deflected or bent at the point where it is crossed by an artery.  Instead of the vein and artery crossing at an acute angle, the vein is pushed aside, taking on a sharp "S-shaped" or "Z-shaped" curve.

Bonnet’s sign is a clinical finding where a retinal vein appears dilated or "banked" distal to an arteriovenous (A/V) crossing. The vein looks swollen or engorged on the side furthest from the optic disc. It often looks like blood is "pooling" because it cannot easily pass under the compressed crossing point.

Copper wiring is a clinical finding on fundoscopy where the retinal arterioles take on a burnished, metallic orange-yellow appearance. The normal bright-red color of the arteriole is replaced by a reddish-brown or orange-yellow metallic sheen.

Silver wiring - The vessel loses its normal red or orange-yellow color and appears as a solid white or silver cord. The central light reflex, which is thin in healthy vessels and broad/metallic in "copper wiring," becomes so wide that it covers the entire width of the vessel.

Abnormal retinal vascular tortuosity refers to an excessive twisting, coiling, or undulating appearance of the retinal arteries or veins on fundoscopy. While some degree of vessel curvature is normal, extreme tortuosity often signals underlying ocular or systemic disease.

Sheathing in fundoscopy refers to a white or grayish-white coating that appears to surround the retinal blood vessels. It is generally a sign of either inflammation or advanced vascular sclerosis. Often looks like a "white cuff" or fuzzy "frosting" around the vessel.

The arterial light reflex (ALR) is the normal reflection of light off the surface of retinal arterioles during fundoscopy. In a healthy eye, the central light streak is thin and bright, usually covering about 1/4 to 1/3 of the total diameter of the vessel.
